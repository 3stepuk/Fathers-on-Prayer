Thinking it through

The Fathers on Prayer — why pray if God already knows?

The question that stopped the early Church in its tracks, and the answers that shaped all Christian prayer after it.

The puzzle

Jesus says the Father knows what you need before you ask. So why ask?

If God already knows, the words seem pointless. If God has already decided, asking seems worse than pointless. It looks like trying to change the mind of someone who cannot be improved.

This was not a small problem for the early Church. It was a serious objection, made by serious people, and the Fathers had to answer it in public.

Origen takes it on

The first to answer it properly was Origen of Alexandria, in a treatise on prayer written around the year 233.

His answer has two parts.

First, God's knowledge is not the same as compulsion. God knows what you will freely do. He does not make you do it. Foreknowledge is not a cause.

Second, and this is the part that matters, prayer is part of how God brings things about. Providence does not work around prayer. It works through it.

Origen sorted motion into three kinds. A stone is moved only from outside. A plant grows from a nature inside it. A person acts from choice. And God, he says, arranges the world so that some things happen because people freely ask for them.

So prayer is not a way of informing God. It is one of the means he uses. That is why the command to pray is not a mistake.

Augustine: Prayer makes the asker

Augustine comes at it from the other side. His question is not how prayer can work. It is what prayer does to the one who prays.

His answer is blunt. God does not need our words. We do.

Prayer, for Augustine, is how desire is formed. We are not born wanting the right things. We learn to want them by asking. The words are not for God's benefit. They are for ours.

He says something stranger still. Prayer stretches the heart. The heart is made larger by longing, and made able to receive what God wants to give. Prayer is not the price of the gift. It is the widening of the vessel.

That is why Augustine can say our prayer is answered even when we do not get what we asked for. What we asked for may have been too small.

The desert: Prayer without words

Then the monks took the argument somewhere else.

In the deserts of Egypt and Palestine, prayer became a discipline of attention. Evagrius, a fourth-century monk, wrote about distraction with a precision that sounds modern. He listed the thoughts that pull the mind away, and he gave them names.

He also described a state beyond words, which he called pure prayer. Not thinking about God, but standing before him.

John Cassian carried this to the West. He described the same climb in stages: the practical life first, then the contemplative. You do not begin with ecstasy. You begin with repentance, and with turning up.

And the monks prayed constantly, or tried to. Out of that came the short repeated prayer that later became the Jesus Prayer. One sentence, said again and again, until it becomes the shape of the mind.

Gregory: The ascent that never ends

Gregory of Nyssa gave this a philosophy.

He argued that God is infinite, and that knowing an infinite God means never finishing. So the Christian life is not a climb that ends on a summit. It is endless progress, which he called epektasis: always stretching forward.

That is a strange idea at first. It means the reward of prayer is more prayer. It also means that boredom in prayer is not a sign that you have finished. It is the ordinary weather of a long road.

Maximus: Prayer as union

Maximus the Confessor pulled the threads together in the seventh century. For him prayer is not one activity among many. It is what the human person is for.

He taught that the mind, purified and settled, can be joined to God. Not by effort alone, and not by a technique. By grace, with effort as the road.

He also insisted on something the earlier writers had implied. Prayer is not private. The Church's liturgy is prayer too, and the whole people prays it together.

Why it still matters

These writers were not answering a puzzle for its own sake. They were protecting two things at once.

They were protecting God's freedom to act, and the reality of human freedom. And they were protecting the plain experience of anyone who has prayed for years: that prayer changes things, and mostly it changes the one praying.

The argument is not over

Philosophy still debates the logic of petitionary prayer, and the problem of an omniscient God and a free creature has not been settled to everyone's satisfaction.

What the Fathers give is not a proof. It is a way of living with the question. They say: pray, and you will learn what prayer is for. That is either a cop-out or the deepest thing in the tradition, and the only way to find out is to try it.

A note on this page

This is a summary of a long and technical tradition. The sources are Origen's On Prayer, Augustine's letters and sermons on prayer, Evagrius and Cassian on the desert life, Gregory of Nyssa on the ascent, and Maximus the Confessor on love and union. If you want to go further, read Origen first: he is the clearest, and the most surprising.
